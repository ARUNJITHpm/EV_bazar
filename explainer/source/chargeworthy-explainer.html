01

Who we are, in one paragraph

Chargeworthy tells you whether your space can make money from EV charging — and if it can, which charging company is the right one to build and run it there.

We are an independent advisor. We do not own charging stations, we do not sell chargers, and we take no cut on hardware. Charging stations are built and run by companies called operators. We are not one of them, and we hold no stake in any of them. That distance is the whole point of us: an operator’s honest answer about your site is still an answer from someone who profits if you say yes.

The one thing we sell is a straight answer, and a document that shows how we reached it. Sometimes that answer is not here, or not yet. Telling you that is not us failing to sell you something — it is the product.

Who this is for

  • Property owners and residents’ associations — apartment complexes, malls, offices and hotels with parking that is currently earning nothing.
  • Businesses and fleets — depots and commercial fleets going electric, who need charging where their vehicles already are.
  • Banks and lenders — a site report detailed enough to lend against, with the working shown rather than asserted.
  • Charging operators — sites that have already been checked, matched to the hardware and economics that suit them.

The problem we exist to solve

Nobody can tell whether a charging station will pay back. Every operator’s pitch sounds confident, and every pitch is made by someone selling. Owners commit twenty to forty lakh rupees on a feeling. We remove the guessing before any money is spent — and we do it with the same method and the same thresholds for every site, so the standard cannot be quietly bent to fit a conclusion.

02

How it works — three steps, and we stop at any of them

  1. Assess the site

    You show us the location — drop a pin on a map or type the address. We check it against 34 fixed things and work out what it would earn. If it does not clear the bar, that is where it ends. You still get the document.

  2. Match the operator

    A highway site and an apartment basement need completely different operators. We compare our partners on the type of charger, the commercial terms, and how much of their own network already sits around you — then name one, with the reasoning shown beside it. Section 05 sets out what decides this, and why it is a different question from the 34.

  3. Install and run

    The operator supplies the charger, the installation and the software that runs it. We stay with you through commissioning. After that the station is theirs to run and yours to earn from.

What it costs you to find out

The first answer is free. Drop a pin and you get, in about half a minute, the single number that decides everything — how busy your chargers would have to be before the site covers its costs. That number is arithmetic, not a prediction, so we can give it away. The full assessment, with the estimate of whether you would actually reach it, is the paid document described in section 06.

03

The one number that decides it

Everything we do turns on one figure: how busy your chargers have to be before the site pays for itself.

Think of a charger the way you would think of a hotel room. The room can be occupied 24 hours a day. It never is. What matters is what share of those hours it is actually paid for — and what share it needs to be paid for before the building stops losing money.

Charging is the same. We work out the fraction you need — the breakeven point — from your costs and your prices:

What you earn on each unit sold — the price you charge, minus the electricity you buy, minus the operator’s share.

What you pay every year no matter what — the cost of the equipment spread over its life, the monthly charge the electricity board bills on your connection size, maintenance, and rent.

Divide the second by the first and you have how many units you must sell each year. As a share of what your chargers could deliver, that is your breakeven point.

This number cannot be wrong the way a forecast can. It is a fact about your costs, not a guess about the future. It is true whether or not anyone’s demand estimate is any good. That is why we give it away free, and why it is the first thing we tell you.

The second number, and why it is a range

Then we estimate how busy your chargers would actually be. That one is uncertain, so we never give it as a single figure. We give a range: a pessimistic case, a middle case and an optimistic case. And the verdict is decided by the pessimistic end — never the middle.

That choice matters. Selling on the middle case is how an advisor ends up with an angry client two years later. If your site only works when everything goes right, we will tell you that is what you are betting on.

We also set the return beside what a fixed deposit would pay you on the same money — an indicative 6.5% at a large bank, with a note telling you to check your own bank’s current rate. If your land plus twenty-five lakh rupees plus five years of your attention beats the bank by very little, that is worth seeing plainly, on the same line.

The three answers you can get

BUILD

Even the pessimistic case clears the breakeven point. The site pays for itself even if demand disappoints.

CONDITIONAL

The middle case clears it; the pessimistic case does not. Building is a bet on things going normally — unless a fleet contract removes the bet.

DON’T BUILD

The site as it stands would not earn back what it costs. The document shows exactly which numbers do not add up, and what would have to change.

04

The 34 things we check, and what each one does to the answer

Every site gets the same 34 checks, in the same five groups. Each group is one source we go and read — which is why the grouping looks the way it does.

Nothing here is a guess. Each one is measured, or taken from a named public record, or marked as unverified — and an unverified one is shown to you, not buried. Factors that argue in your favour are listed exactly as openly as the ones that argue against.

Group 1 · 12 checks · read from the OpenStreetMap road layer

Getting in and out

A charger nobody can conveniently turn into is a charger nobody uses. This is the group that most often quietly kills a site that looked good on a map.

What we check What it does to the answer
Road class A national highway carries far more potential customers past your gate than a residential lane. The bigger the road, the larger the pool your demand is drawn from.
Distance from main road Every extra metre off the main road is another reason not to turn in. Sitting within about 100 m of a major road counts in your favour; beyond roughly 300 m counts against.
Carriageway direction served On a divided road you only get the traffic on your side. Serving one direction rather than both can halve the flow that can actually reach you.
Sub-road access A second way in from a side road lets drivers reach you without fighting the main carriageway, and gives you customers who were not passing at all.
Median or divider A solid median means traffic coming the other way cannot turn in without a long detour. Almost nobody makes that detour to charge.
Sight line A driver has to see the station early enough to slow down and turn. A short sight line means people notice you as they pass you.
Turning radius A tight turn shuts out anything larger than a car — vans, small trucks, anything towing. That can remove a whole segment of your market.
Entry and exit width A narrow entry creates a queue at the gate. A queue at the gate is a customer who keeps driving.
Frontage width How much of your land the road can actually see. Wide frontage makes the site readable at speed; narrow frontage makes it a surprise.
Traffic count How many vehicles pass on an average day — the size of the pool everything else draws from. India has no free national source for this, so where we cannot get a real count we say so and reason from road class, junctions and what is nearby instead of inventing a figure.
Dominant flow direction Traffic that is heavier inbound in the morning behaves differently from traffic that is heavier outbound in the evening. It decides when your chargers are busy.
Peak hour timing When the flow peaks decides whether your busiest hours land on top of your most expensive electricity hours — which changes what you earn per unit.

Group 2 · 4 checks · read from VAHAN, the government vehicle register

Whether there are electric vehicles here at all

Passing traffic only helps if some of it is electric. These come from the national registration database, district by district.

What we check What it does to the answer
EV registrations How many electric vehicles are actually registered in your district. Above roughly 10,000 counts in your favour; below about 3,000 counts against — there simply are not enough vehicles to fill a charger yet.
Registration mix Most of India’s EVs are two- and three-wheelers, and they do not fill a fast four-wheeler charger. A district that is heavily two-wheeler needs a different kind of station, not a bigger one.
Fleet operators within 10 km The single biggest de-risker in Indian charging. A contract with a fleet — buses, cabs, three-wheeler aggregators — that guarantees a minimum offtake turns a bet into an income. A nearby fleet alone can move a site from don’t to build.
Distance to nearest city How far away the local pool of EV drivers actually lives and works, which sets how often they pass you rather than someone closer to them.

Group 3 · 8 checks · read from your state electricity regulator’s tariff order

Power, and what it costs you every month

This is where good-looking sites most often fail on arithmetic. Several of these are bills you pay whether or not you sell a single unit.

What we check What it does to the answer
Tariff order Which published order your connection is billed under. It sets the price of every unit you buy, and therefore what you keep on every unit you sell.
Demand charges A monthly bill based on the size of your connection, payable whether you sell anything or not. This is frequently the largest fixed cost of the whole station — a few hundred rupees per kVA per month becomes two to four lakh a year before the first customer.
Sanctioned load The connection size you contract for. Too large and you burn money every month on the charge above; a smaller connection with managed peaks can save lakhs a year. We recommend the size rather than simply accept the one you ask for.
Transformer distance Cabling from the nearest transformer costs roughly ₹2,000 a metre. Three hundred metres is about six lakh rupees spent before a charger is even installed.
Transformer spare capacity If the existing transformer already has room for your peak, you avoid building a new one — roughly two lakh rupees that drops straight out of the capital cost.
Grid outage hours Hours the supply is down are hours you cannot sell. This is often the one thing we cannot source for a given location — and when we cannot, we mark it unverified rather than assume it is zero, because assuming zero flatters the site.
New connection cost The one-time cost of getting the supply you need, paid before a single unit is sold. It goes into the capital you have to earn back.
State subsidy applicability State EV policy and central schemes can cut the capital cost materially, and capital is what the payback period is measured against. A subsidy you qualify for shortens payback directly.

Group 4 · 7 checks · read from the OpenStreetMap places layer, within 1 km

The site itself, and what surrounds it

A driver charging a car is a person standing somewhere for twenty to forty minutes. What they can do while they wait changes how often they choose you.

What we check What it does to the answer
Plot area Whether the layout works at all — bays, room to queue, room to turn. Area does not earn anything by itself, but too little of it rules the site out.
Parking bays Bays decide how many cars can charge at once, which sets the ceiling on what the site can possibly earn. More bays also spread the fixed monthly costs across more capacity, which pushes the breakeven point down.
Canopy feasibility Shade keeps drivers comfortable in an Indian summer, and a canopy is where rooftop solar goes. Solar changes what you keep on every unit sold, which can move the verdict on its own.
Amenities within walking distance Somewhere to sit, eat or shop. A mall holds a driver for forty-five minutes; a fuel pump holds nobody. Longer comfortable waits mean fuller chargers.
Mobile network coverage Chargers authorise and take payment over the mobile network. Weak signal means failed payments and a driver who does not come back.
Night lighting People will not stop alone at an unlit site after dark. Poor lighting quietly removes the night hours from what your chargers can earn.
Land or lease cost Rent is a fixed monthly cost in exactly the same line as the demand charge — payable in full in a month when nobody charges at all.

Group 5 · 3 checks · read from the competitor inventory, 10 km radius

Who else is already charging nearby

Demand is shared. Two stations in the same catchment do not each get the whole of it.

What we check What it does to the answer
Competitor distance How far the nearest existing charger is. Under about 500 m counts against you; more than about 2 km counts in your favour, because a driver will not detour past a working charger to reach yours.
Competitor density at 3 / 5 / 10 km How many chargers already share your pool of drivers. Three or more within 3 km, or five or more within 5 km, counts against the site — this is the single most common reason we say don’t build.
Announced stations Stations announced but not yet built. A catchment that is clear today can be crowded by the time yours is commissioned, and your payback is measured over years, not months.

One colour, one meaning. Anything we could not verify is marked in a single colour used for nothing else in the entire document. If you see it, it means “we could not confirm this” — never “look here, this is important”. A gap gets reported, never quietly filled.

05

Choosing who runs it — and why it changes the money

A site that clears the bar raises a second question, and it is not the same question. You own the land and the electricity connection. Somebody else owns the charger, the app and the customer. Which somebody makes a real difference to what you earn.

The station is not yours to run

A charging company — an operator — supplies the machine, installs it, keeps it working and runs the app drivers use to find it and pay for it. You supply the ground and the power, and take a share. So the operator you sign with decides two things at once: what share you keep, and who can find you at all. Most drivers do not go looking for a charger. They open one app, and they go where it sends them.

Why the same charger nearby can mean two different things

Picture two fuel pumps a kilometre apart. If they carry different brands, they compete for the cars going past. If they carry the same brand, something else happens as well: that brand’s own customers simply divide between them, and the second pump makes the first one quieter. Nobody new arrived and nobody left. The same drivers were split in two.

Chargers work the same way. A charger a kilometre from you, run by company A, is competition for everybody. But if you also sign with company A, it is competition and a split — company A’s app now has two places to send the same drivers, and one of them is yours. Sign with company B instead, and that neighbour goes back to being ordinary competition.

So the same neighbour counts differently depending on who you sign with. This is the part that gets missed. A location can look crowded and still be a good one — for the right operator. It can look clear and still disappoint, if the operator you picked already has three of its own machines around the corner.

The six things that decide the operator

What we look at What it does to your answer
Their stations in your district How many drivers around you already have this company’s app on their phone. A company with no presence near you has no local audience to send you, whatever its terms say.
Their stations in your state How far away the nearest engineer is when a charger stops working. A network with three sites in the whole state cannot reach you quickly, and a charger that is down earns nothing at all.
Their own stations close to you The split described above. Fewer is better. This is the one factor that can make the biggest, best-known network the wrong choice for your particular plot.
Whether their chargers suit the vehicles near you A district full of electric two-wheelers and a network built for fast car charging is a mismatch no revenue share repairs. We already count what is registered around you; this checks it against what they install.
Whether their app connects to other apps Some networks let a driver using a rival’s app find and pay at their chargers. That widens the audience for your plug, and it partly makes up for a company being small in your area.
Their commercial terms Their share of each unit sold, any yearly platform fee, whether the machine is included or bought by you, who pays for maintenance, and how many years you are tied in. These decide how much of the money reaches you.

How this reaches you

Part 06 of your report is the operator comparison. The whole calculation is run again, once for each operator, using that operator’s own terms — so you see the same site with three different answers rather than one average. The six things above sit in the same table, beside the money, and are never mixed into a single score. Trading a better return against better service is your decision, and one combined number would quietly make it for you.

What is counted today, and what is not. How many stations each company runs near you, in your district and in your state, comes from our own inventory of chargers — counted, not estimated. How reliably each company’s chargers actually work, and how busy their nearby machines are, is something we are still building the means to observe; until it runs, the report says so rather than guessing. Commercial terms are never invented: where an operator has not given us its terms in writing, the table says so and leaves the row empty.

The rule we hold ourselves to here

We are connected to a charging company ourselves, and we say so on the last page of every report. In this comparison it is listed like any other, under the same rules — including the split. If the nearest machine to your gate turns out to be one of ours, that counts against us in our own document, in print, where you can see it. A comparison that could never embarrass the people who wrote it is not a comparison.

07

Why you can believe the answer

Trust is a design problem, not a promise. Every item below is a rule built into how the assessment is produced — not an intention we hold.

We do not benefit from a yes.
We are not a charging operator. We own no stations, hold no stake in any operator or charger manufacturer, and take no margin on hardware. The assessment fee is the same whether the answer is build or don’t build.
The cautious case decides, not the hopeful one.
The verdict is read off the pessimistic end of the estimated range. A site that only works if everything goes right is told it is a bet, in those words.
Ranges, never single numbers.
Any figure that depends on the future is given as a range. A single number would claim a precision nobody has earned, and precision is exactly what makes a wrong forecast convincing.
The rules are printed before your data.
Part 03 of the report states the thresholds ahead of part 04's facts. A standard shown after the evidence can never prove it was not fitted to it.
Every factor is shown, including the ones in your favour.
All 34 appear with the direction they push. Reporting only what supports the conclusion is how an advisory quietly becomes a sales funnel. And each direction is a symbol and a word, so the document still reads correctly when it is photocopied in black and white for a bank file.
Not knowing something makes the answer less certain, never more convenient.
A missing input widens the estimated range. It is never filled with a helpful assumption that happens to flatter the site — and it is marked so you can see it was missing.
No computer picks the money.
The one thing we model is how many units a charger would sell in a day. Every rupee — payback, return, cashflow — comes from fixed arithmetic applied to that. This is what makes a wrong number traceable: it is a bad electricity tariff or a bad map pin, and it can be found and named, not shrugged at as “the model”.
What would change the verdict is written down.
With the level each condition would have to reach. If a fleet contract or a lower connection size would flip a no into a yes, the report says so instead of leaving you to wonder.
Your report is frozen the day it is made.
It is stored exactly as generated and shown back to you unchanged, forever. It is never quietly recalculated against later data. What you were told is what you can still point at.
The conflict is stated inside the document.
We do earn a fee when a site goes ahead with a partner operator. That is written into the report itself, beside the share of sites we advised against — because a reader who can see the incentive and the record together can judge for themselves.
We publish how often we say no.
An advisor who says build almost every time is a sales channel, and the number makes that visible to everyone. Publishing it is what stops us from becoming one.
08

What we do not claim

A list of our own limits belongs in the same document as our conclusions. These are things a confident advisor could easily pretend to know.

  • Which side of a divided road you are on. Being on the wrong side can lose roughly half your passing traffic, and matching the two carriageways properly is work we have not done yet. Where it matters, the report says the question is open rather than guessing an answer.
  • How long it actually takes to drive to you. We measure distance on a map, not driving time through real traffic.
  • How busy the chargers near you really are. We know where they are and how powerful they are. How full they run is something only long-term measurement can tell, and we are building that record now — we are not claiming it before it exists.
  • A real traffic count, everywhere. There is no free national source of traffic counts in India. Where we do not have one, we reason from road class, junctions and nearby destinations, and we say that is what we did.
  • Every state, equally. Coverage is built state by state, because each one’s electricity tariff orders have to be read and typed in by a person. Outside a covered state we tell you so and add your location to the queue rather than produce a report we cannot stand behind. The queue is genuinely how we choose what to cover next.
  • A single “site score” out of 100. We deliberately do not produce one. It would be easy to market and impossible to defend, and it would hide exactly the disagreements between factors that you most need to see.

About the numbers on our own website. Some figures we would like to publish about ourselves — how many sites we have assessed, what share we advised against, how often our estimates proved right — are shown in square brackets until our own records can back them. A bracket means “we do not have this yet”, and removing one is a decision a person makes with evidence in hand. We would rather show you a visible gap than a confident number nobody checked.

09

The words we use

Word In plain English
Operator The company that supplies the charger, installs it, runs the software behind it and fixes it when it breaks. You are not one; we are not one either.
Roaming An arrangement that lets a driver using one company’s app charge at another company’s machine. It widens the pool of drivers who can use your plug.
Unit / kWh What you buy electricity in and sell charging in. It is the same “unit” that is on your house bill.
kVA, and why it is not kW kW is how fast a charger delivers power. kVA is the size of connection the electricity board bills you for — and it is bigger than the kW figure. A 60 kW charger needs about 67 kVA. Confusing the two is how a business plan ends up short by lakhs a year.
Utilisation The share of the time your chargers are actually being paid for, out of all the time they could be.
Breakeven utilisation How busy they need to be before the site stops losing money. The number this whole product is built around.
Pessimistic / middle / optimistic Our three cases. Roughly: it goes worse than expected, it goes as expected, it goes better. The verdict reads the first one.
Payback period How many years until the money you put in has come back out. Not the same as profit — it ignores what happens after.
Demand charge A monthly electricity bill based on your connection size, not on what you use. You pay it in a month when nobody charges at all.
Dwell How long a driver is comfortably held at a place. A mall holds a driver; a fuel pump does not.
Fleet anchor A contract with a fleet that guarantees a minimum amount of charging. The strongest single thing that can turn an uncertain site into a safe one.
